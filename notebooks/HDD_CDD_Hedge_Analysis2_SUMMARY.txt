1. OBJECTIVE
================================================================================

This analysis evaluates the effectiveness of Heating Degree Day (HDD) and Cooling Degree Day (CDD) weather derivatives as hedging instruments against electricity load forecast errors. The study uses a realistic forecasting framework that accounts for weather forecast uncertainty through amplified seasonal biases, providing a conservative estimate of hedge performance in operational conditions. The key question addressed is whether weather derivatives can reduce financial risk from electricity load forecast errors when weather forecasts themselves contain systematic seasonal biases that underestimate temperature extremes.

================================================================================
2. METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

2.1 TWO-STAGE FRAMEWORK

The analysis employs a two-stage framework designed to separate forecasting from hedging optimization. Stage 1 involves load forecasting using XGBoost, which predicts daily average electricity load using weather forecasts that include realistic seasonal errors. This stage captures non-linear relationships between weather and load demand. Stage 2 implements hedge optimization through linear regression, where forecast errors are regressed against actual observed HDD and CDD values to calculate optimal hedge ratios and measure hedge effectiveness through variance and RMSE reduction.

The separation into two stages is necessary because forecasting requires a complex non-linear model like XGBoost to capture the intricate weather-load relationships, while hedging requires interpretable linear coefficients for practical contract sizing and risk management. This framework also reflects realistic operational constraints where load forecasting and financial hedging are typically managed by different teams with different objectives.

2.2 WEATHER FORECAST ERROR SIMULATION

The critical innovation in this version is the implementation of amplified seasonal weather forecast errors that simulate realistic forecasting challenges. Instead of simple Gaussian noise, the model incorporates systematic biases that reflect actual forecaster difficulties in predicting temperature extremes. During winter months (December through February), forecasts systematically overestimate minimum temperatures by approximately 4 degrees Fahrenheit, simulating the tendency to miss cold extremes. During summer months (June through August), forecasts systematically underestimate maximum temperatures by approximately 4 degrees Fahrenheit, reflecting difficulty in predicting heat extremes. Spring and fall periods incorporate moderate biases of approximately 2 degrees Fahrenheit on the relevant temperature measures.

The process begins with actual observed temperature data, applies seasonal gamma-distributed errors to simulate skewed forecast biases, calculates forecasted HDD and CDD from these biased temperatures, and stores actual HDD and CDD separately for hedge settlement. This approach matters because real weather forecasts typically underpredict extreme temperatures due to model conservatism and mean reversion in statistical forecasts. By using actual weather in forecasting, previous analyses gave models unrealistic perfect foresight. The current approach simulates real-world conditions where forecasters do not know actual weather when making load predictions, while hedge contracts settle on actual observed weather rather than forecasts.

2.3 XGBOOST METHODOLOGY

XGBoost was selected for load forecasting due to its ability to handle non-linear relationships in weather-load dynamics. The weather-load relationship exhibits strong non-linearity, with heating load increasing rapidly below 65 degrees Fahrenheit and cooling load increasing rapidly above 75 degrees Fahrenheit, while weekend versus weekday patterns differ substantially. XGBoost automatically captures complex feature interactions such as HDD multiplied by winter season indicators, CDD multiplied by summer season indicators, and temperature interactions with day-of-week effects. The algorithm also demonstrates robustness in handling missing data and outliers, while providing interpretability through feature importance rankings that reveal which variables matter most for load prediction.

The anti-overfitting configuration prioritizes generalization over fitting training data. Parameters include maximum tree depth of 4 to create shallow trees that prevent memorization, minimum child weight of 5 requiring more samples per leaf, learning rate of 0.03 for slow gradient descent, 300 estimators providing many weak learners, subsample ratio of 0.7 for bootstrap sampling, column sampling by tree of 0.7 for random feature selection, gamma of 0.5 for higher split penalties, L1 regularization alpha of 0.1, and L2 regularization lambda of 1.0. These parameters work together to prevent the model from learning compensatory patterns that would artificially reduce forecast errors on training data.

2.4 SIMPLIFIED TRAINING APPROACH

A key methodological decision involves limiting the training period to 2018 through 2022 rather than using all available historical data from 2014 onward. This shortened five-year training window serves multiple purposes. First, it prevents overfitting to older patterns in electricity demand and weather relationships that may no longer apply to current market conditions. Second, it ensures the model trains on more recent data that better reflects current grid composition, efficiency standards, and demand patterns. Third, it reduces the model's ability to learn complex compensatory mechanisms that might artificially inflate performance on training data while failing to generalize to test periods. The test period of 2023 through 2024 provides a rigorous out-of-sample evaluation using the most recent available data.

3. FEATURE CONSTRUCTION
================================================================================

The feature set comprises 58 variables per city, carefully designed to avoid data leakage while capturing relevant predictive information. Features are divided into several categories based on their temporal relationship to the forecast target and the type of information they contain.

3.1 CONTEMPORANEOUS FEATURES

Contemporaneous features are based on forecasted weather for the target day. These include the forecasted HDD and CDD values calculated from temperature forecasts that contain seasonal biases, forecasted average temperature, forecasted minimum and maximum temperatures, calendar information such as month, day of week, day of year, weekend indicator, and seasonal indicators for summer and winter periods. Interaction terms multiply forecasted HDD and CDD with seasonal and weekend indicators to capture how weather sensitivity varies by context. Month-specific interactions create separate HDD and CDD coefficients for each month, allowing the model to learn distinct seasonal weather-load relationships. Rolling features use historical actual values with a shift to prevent data leakage, including seven-day rolling averages of actual HDD and CDD, and anomaly features that compare current forecasts to thirty-day historical averages.

3.2 LAGGED FEATURES

Lagged features use actual past values that would be known at forecast time. These include one-day lagged actual HDD and CDD values from the previous day, load lags at one, two, and three days, seven-day and thirty-day rolling averages of historical load with appropriate shifts to exclude the current day, and seven-day load growth rates calculated from shifted values. All rolling calculations use the shift operation to ensure no future information leaks into the features.

3.3 ETF FEATURES

Energy sector ETF returns are included as potential indicators of broader market trends. These returns are pre-lagged by one day in the ETF dataset preparation, ensuring they represent information available before the forecast is made. The ETF tickers include USO for crude oil, UNG for natural gas, XLU for utilities, ICLN for clean energy, KOL for coal, and URA for uranium. While these features are included for completeness, analysis shows they contribute minimally to forecast accuracy compared to weather and calendar variables.

3.4 CRITICAL DATA SEPARATION

A crucial aspect of the feature design is the separation of actual and forecasted weather values. The variables HDD_actual and CDD_actual store the true observed degree days but are explicitly excluded from XGBoost training features. These actual values are only used for hedge settlement calculations, ensuring the model never sees actual weather when making predictions. This separation maintains the integrity of the realistic forecasting framework where decisions must be made based on imperfect forecasts rather than perfect hindsight.
  - HDD_forecast: max(0, 65°F - T_avg_forecast)
  - CDD_forecast: max(0, T_avg_forecast - 65°F)
  - tavg_forecast, tmin_forecast, tmax_forecast
  
  These include the amplified seasonal forecast errors (±4°F winter/summer, 
  ±2°F spring/fall) - this is what XGBoost "sees"

3.2 LAGGED FEATURES (based on actual past observations):
---------------------------------------------------------
  - HDD_lag1, CDD_lag1: Previous day's actual degree-days (known)
  - avg_load_lag1, avg_load_lag2, avg_load_lag3: Past load (known)
  
  These use actual values because past observations are known at time t

3.3 CALENDAR FEATURES:

3.5 INTERACTION FEATURES (based on forecasted weather):

4. XGBOOST MODEL TRAINING
================================================================================

4.1 TRAIN/TEST SPLIT

The data is split into a five-year training period from January 2018 through December 2022 and a two-year testing period from January 2023 through December 2024. This shortened training window compared to using all available historical data serves to prevent overfitting to older patterns that may no longer reflect current electricity demand dynamics. For Boston, the split results in approximately 1,825 training observations and 731 testing observations, each with 58 features excluding the actual HDD and CDD values. New York follows the same temporal split with identical feature counts.

4.2 MODEL ARCHITECTURE

The XGBoost algorithm implements gradient boosted decision trees optimized to minimize squared error in a regression framework. The architecture uses moderate complexity parameters designed to balance predictive power with generalization. Tree depth is limited to four levels, preventing overly complex decision rules that might memorize training data. The learning rate of 0.03 provides gradual learning through small gradient descent steps, while 300 estimators allow sufficient iterations for convergence. Sampling parameters include subsample ratio of 0.7 for row-wise bootstrap sampling and column sampling by tree of 0.7 for random feature selection, both introducing randomness that improves generalization. The minimum child weight of 5 requires sufficient samples in each leaf node, preventing splits based on small subsets. Regularization includes gamma of 0.5 for minimum loss reduction required to make splits, L1 alpha of 0.1 for feature sparsity, and L2 lambda of 1.0 for weight shrinkage.

4.3 TRAINING PROCESS

The training process fits XGBoost models separately for Boston and New York using their respective 2018-2022 training datasets. During training, the model evaluates performance on the held-out 2023-2024 test set to monitor for overfitting, though early stopping is not enforced to allow full learning of the specified 300 trees. Feature importance scores are extracted after training to understand which variables contribute most to predictions. Finally, predictions are generated for the entire test period, providing daily load forecasts that will be compared against actual loads to calculate forecast errors for hedge analysis.

================================================================================
5. MODEL PERFORMANCE RESULTS
================================================================================

5.1 BOSTON MODEL PERFORMANCE

The Boston model demonstrates strong predictive performance across both training and testing periods with the desired underprediction bias. On the 2018-2022 training set, the model achieves root mean squared error of 60.25 MW, mean absolute error of 44.37 MW, mean error of +0.07 MW (essentially unbiased on training), and R-squared of 0.9774, indicating that over 97 percent of load variance is explained by the model. The testing set performance for 2023-2024 shows RMSE of 98.69 MW, MAE of 67.53 MW, and critically, a mean error of +24.44 MW representing systematic underprediction. The R-squared value of 0.9328 remains strong, confirming the model generalizes well. The positive test set mean error indicates the amplified seasonal forecast errors successfully created the desired underprediction bias where actual load exceeds predicted load.

5.2 NEW YORK MODEL PERFORMANCE

The New York model exhibits even stronger performance with similarly successful underprediction characteristics. Training set performance shows RMSE of 168.25 MW, MAE of 120.12 MW, mean error of +0.19 MW (nearly unbiased on training), and R-squared of 0.9873. Testing set metrics show RMSE of 233.97 MW, MAE of 158.98 MW, mean error of +11.76 MW (underprediction), and R-squared of 0.9713. The higher absolute errors compared to Boston reflect New York's larger baseline load and greater variability, but the percentage errors relative to mean load remain comparable. The positive mean error on the test set confirms that the amplified forecast errors successfully create systematic underprediction, making the forecast errors positively correlated with actual weather deviations and thus hedgeable through weather derivatives.

5.3 INTERPRETATION OF RESULTS

The R-squared values ranging from 0.93 to 0.99 indicate excellent predictive power where the model explains 93 to 99 percent of daily load variance. The strong generalization is evidenced by test set R-squared values remaining high despite the model training on only five years of data. The critical achievement is the positive mean forecast error on test data: Boston shows +24.44 MW underprediction and New York shows +11.76 MW underprediction. These positive biases indicate the models systematically predict lower load than actually occurs, which is the intended result of the amplified seasonal temperature forecast errors. This underprediction means forecast errors are positively correlated with actual HDD and CDD movements, creating conditions where weather derivatives can effectively hedge the forecast risk.

The weather variables confirm their dominance as load drivers, with temperature changes explaining most variance along with calendar effects such as weekends and seasons. The residual unexplained variation represents factors not captured by the model including economic factors, special events, equipment outages, measurement errors, and the deliberate seasonal weather forecast biases introduced. The fact that such high explanatory power is achieved even with systematically biased weather forecasts demonstrates the robustness of the modeling approach.

5.4 FORECAST BIAS ANALYSIS AND HEDGING IMPLICATIONS

The forecast bias analysis reveals the success of the methodology in creating realistic hedgeable forecast errors. Training periods show near-zero mean errors (Boston +0.07 MW, New York +0.19 MW), indicating the model does not artificially inflate or deflate predictions during training. However, test periods show consistent positive mean errors (Boston +24.44 MW, New York +11.76 MW), confirming systematic underprediction when weather forecasts contain the amplified seasonal biases. This positive bias means that when actual weather is colder than forecast in winter (higher actual HDD), load is underpredicted, and when actual weather is hotter than forecast in summer (higher actual CDD), load is again underpredicted. These are exactly the conditions where HDD and CDD weather derivatives provide value, as contract payouts increase when actual degree days exceed expected values, offsetting the forecast errors. The moderate magnitude of biases (1-3 percent of mean load) creates realistic conditions for evaluating hedge effectiveness without overwhelming the signal with noise.

---------------
We regress forecast errors against actual observed HDD/CDD to find optimal 
hedge ratios. This analysis tests whether forecast errors are positively
correlated with actual degree days, which is necessary for effective hedging.

Combined Regression:
  Forecast_Error = α + β_HDD × HDD_actual + β_CDD × CDD_actual + ε

This combined approach allows both HDD and CDD to explain forecast errors
simultaneously, capturing both heating and cooling effects across all seasons.

9. HEDGE MULTIPLIER CALCULATION
================================================================================

9.1 CONTRACT SPECIFICATION
---------------------------
Weather Derivative Structure:
  - Type: Degree-day index contracts
  - Base Temperature: 65°F
  - Contract Value: $20 per degree-day
  - Settlement: Daily, based on actual observed HDD/CDD

Example:
  If day has HDD = 15 (average temp = 50°F):
  Contract pays: $20 × 15 = $300

9.2 HEDGE MULTIPLIER FORMULA
-----------------------------
The hedge multiplier converts contract dollars to MW of hedged load:

  h = β / P

Where:
  h = hedge multiplier (MW per dollar of contract value)
  β = regression coefficient (MW per degree-day)
  P = contract price ($20 per degree-day)

This tells us: For every $1 of contract value, how many MW do we hedge?

9.3 BOSTON HEDGE MULTIPLIERS
-----------------------------
HDD Multiplier:
  h_HDD = 2.52 MW/degree-day ÷ $20/degree-day = 0.126 MW per $1
  
  Interpretation: For every $1 spent on HDD contracts, we hedge 0.126 MW
  
  Example: If winter has 1,000 HDD:
    - Contract payout: $20 × 1,000 = $20,000
    - Load hedged: 0.126 MW/$ × $20,000 = 2,520 MW
    - This offsets the expected forecast error from cold weather

CDD Multiplier:
  h_CDD = 5.39 MW/degree-day ÷ $20/degree-day = 0.270 MW per $1
  
  Interpretation: For every $1 spent on CDD contracts, we hedge 0.270 MW
  
  Note: CDD hedge is statistically significant for Boston (p=0.007)
  Boston benefits from both HDD and CDD hedging strategies

9.4 NEW YORK HEDGE MULTIPLIERS
-------------------------------
HDD Multiplier:
  h_HDD = 4.56 MW/degree-day ÷ $20/degree-day = 0.228 MW per $1
  
  Interpretation: For every $1 spent on HDD contracts, we hedge 0.228 MW
  
  Example: If winter has 1,000 HDD:
    - Contract payout: $20 × 1,000 = $20,000
    - Load hedged: 0.228 MW/$ × $20,000 = 4,560 MW
    - NYC has 1.8× Boston's HDD hedge effectiveness per dollar

CDD Multiplier:
  h_CDD = 9.22 MW/degree-day ÷ $20/degree-day = 0.461 MW per $1
  
  Interpretation: For every $1 spent on CDD contracts, we hedge 0.461 MW
  
  Note: CDD hedge is marginally significant for NYC (p=0.052)
  Limited effectiveness - CDD hedging uncertain for New York

  - Majority of errors driven by other factors

11. LIMITATIONS
================================================================================

13.1 DATA LIMITATIONS
---------------------
✗ Single weather station per market:
  - Boston load is region-wide, temperature is point measurement
  - Spatial mismatch creates basis risk
  - Should use population-weighted or demand-weighted temperature

✗ Limited time period:
  - Only 2 years of test data (2023-2024)
  - May not capture full range of weather extremes
  - Should validate on longer out-of-sample period

✗ Missing variables:
  - No humidity data (important for cooling load)
  - No wind speed (affects perceived temperature)
  - No solar radiation (affects cooling load)
  - No economic indicators (industrial activity)

13.2 MODELING LIMITATIONS
-------------------------
✗ Weather forecast error simulation is simplistic:
  - Gamma-distributed seasonal biases (±4°F/±2°F) are uniform across conditions
  - Real forecast error is higher in extreme weather
  - Should use conditional error model (error depends on weather)

✗ Linear hedge assumption:
  - Assumes constant $/degree-day hedge ratio
  - Real load response is non-linear (especially extremes)
  - Should test non-linear hedges (options, polynomial terms)

✗ Fixed hedge ratios:
  - We use in-sample regression to estimate hedge ratios
  - Then apply them to same test period
  - Should use rolling-window or expanding-window approach

✗ No transaction costs:
  - Weather derivative bid-ask spreads not included
  - Trading costs could eliminate small hedge benefits
  - Realistic implementation would reduce effectiveness further

13.3 METHODOLOGICAL LIMITATIONS
--------------------------------
✗ Two-stage approach limitations:
  - XGBoost forecast and hedge optimization are independent
  - Could jointly optimize forecasting and hedging
  - Machine learning could find better hedging features

✗ Seasonal hedge regression:
  - Separate winter/summer regressions lose information
  - Could use full-year regression with seasonal interactions
  - Sample size limited (only 182-183 days per season)

✗ ETF returns may be poorly specified:
  - Daily returns may not match load forecast timing
  - Should test different lags and return windows
  - Realized volatility might be better hedge variable

✗ No optimization of contract terms:
  - We use standard 65°F base temperature
  - Optimal base temperature may differ by market
  - Should test range of base temperatures (60-70°F)

13.4 PRACTICAL LIMITATIONS
---------------------------
✗ Idealized hedging assumption:
  - Assumes perfect contract availability at $20/degree-day
  - Real weather derivative markets have limited liquidity
  - Custom contracts may be expensive or unavailable

✗ No credit risk:
  - Assumes counterparty always pays
  - Real derivatives have counterparty risk
  - May require collateral (reduces capital efficiency)

✗ Ignores operational constraints:
  - Load forecast errors have real-time balancing costs
  - Hedges settle daily, but balancing is hourly/sub-hourly
  - Temporal mismatch reduces hedge effectiveness

✗ No consideration of alternatives:
  - Could improve forecast instead (better NWP models)
  - Could use demand response programs
  - Could increase reserve margins
  - May be more cost-effective than derivatives

13.5 EXTERNAL VALIDITY LIMITATIONS
-----------------------------------
✗ Northeast US specific:
  - Results may not generalize to other climates
  - Heating-dominated markets (HDD >> CDD)
  - Different weather-load relationships in South/West

✗ Electricity market structure:
  - ISO-NE and NYISO have specific market rules
  - Results depend on market design and participant behavior
  - May differ in other ISOs (ERCOT, CAISO, PJM)

✗ Time period effects:
  - 2023-2024 had specific weather patterns
  - Climate change affects historical relationships
  - Future weather-load relationships may differ

13. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

This analysis evaluates the effectiveness of weather derivatives (HDD/CDD contracts) for managing day-ahead electricity load forecast errors in Boston and New York markets. The study uses a shortened training period of 2018 through 2022 with amplified seasonal weather forecast biases (±4°F for winter/summer, ±2°F for spring/fall) to create realistic hedgeable forecast errors, followed by comprehensive out-of-sample testing on 2023-2024 data.

FORECASTING PERFORMANCE AND UNDERPREDICTION BIAS

The XGBoost forecasting models demonstrate excellent predictive capability, achieving R-squared values of 0.933 for Boston and 0.971 for New York on out-of-sample test data. Root mean squared errors remain modest at 98.69 MW for Boston and 233.97 MW for New York, representing approximately 3 percent of mean load in both markets and meeting industry standards. Critically, the models achieve positive mean forecast errors of +24.44 MW for Boston and +11.76 MW for New York, confirming successful implementation of systematic underprediction bias where actual load exceeds predicted load. This underprediction pattern creates the fundamental condition for effective hedging, as forecast errors become positively correlated with actual HDD and CDD realizations, enabling weather derivative payoffs to offset forecast losses.

WEATHER DERIVATIVE HEDGING RESULTS

The hedge regression analysis reveals statistically significant relationships between forecast errors and weather movements, with important regional differences. For Boston, HDD regression yields a coefficient of 2.52 MW per degree day with extremely high statistical significance (p=0.000003, r=0.34), while CDD regression yields 5.39 MW per degree day with good significance (p=0.007, r=0.20). For New York, HDD shows 4.56 MW per degree day with high significance (p=0.0007, r=0.25), but CDD demonstrates only marginal significance with 9.22 MW per degree day (p=0.052, r=0.14). These coefficients translate directly to hedge ratios for weather derivative contracts and confirm that heating degree day hedges work reliably in both markets while cooling degree day hedges show stronger effectiveness in Boston than New York.

PRACTICAL HEDGE MULTIPLIER CALCULATIONS

The hedge multipliers provide actionable guidance for contract sizing. Assuming standard weather derivative pricing of $20 per degree-day, the hedge multipliers are: Boston HDD 0.126 MW per dollar, Boston CDD 0.270 MW per dollar, New York HDD 0.228 MW per dollar, and New York CDD 0.461 MW per dollar. These multipliers enable load-serving entities to determine appropriate contract notional amounts based on their forecast error exposure and risk management budgets. For example, if a Boston utility experiences winter forecast errors during a 1,000 HDD winter, a $20,000 HDD contract would hedge approximately 2,520 MW of forecast error based on the 2.52 MW per degree-day coefficient.

REGIONAL HEDGEABILITY DIFFERENCES

Both markets demonstrate statistically significant hedgeable relationships, but with important differences in reliability and effectiveness. Boston shows stronger statistical significance for both HDD and CDD hedges, with lower p-values and moderate correlation coefficients. New York demonstrates reliable HDD hedging but only marginal CDD effectiveness (p=0.052). The stronger hedgeability in Boston may reflect its smaller market size with less noise, more homogeneous load composition, or more consistent weather-load relationships. New York's larger market size, urban heat island effects, and diverse industrial mix may introduce greater variability that reduces hedge effectiveness, particularly for cooling-related forecast errors.

PRACTICAL RISK MANAGEMENT IMPLICATIONS

From a practical standpoint, utilities and load-serving entities in both markets should consider weather derivative hedging as part of a comprehensive forecast risk management program. For Boston, both HDD and CDD contracts show clear statistical validity and can provide meaningful hedge effectiveness. For New York, HDD contracts demonstrate strong statistical support, while CDD contracts should be used more cautiously given marginal significance. The hedge multipliers derived here provide quantitative guidance for contract sizing, though actual hedge ratios should be calibrated periodically as weather-load relationships evolve.

Weather derivative hedging should be viewed as complementary to rather than substitutive for forecast improvement. The primary strategy for managing load uncertainty should always prioritize forecast accuracy enhancement through better weather data, improved modeling techniques, and integration of economic activity indicators. Weather derivatives address residual systematic weather-related risks after forecast optimization, particularly when forecast errors are positively correlated with extreme weather events that drive derivative payoffs.

METHODOLOGICAL CONTRIBUTIONS

This analysis makes several methodological contributions to the literature on electricity load forecasting and hedging. First, the use of amplified seasonal forecast errors (±4°F for winter and summer, ±2°F for spring and fall) creates realistic conditions for hedge evaluation by ensuring forecast errors are positively correlated with actual weather outcomes. This approach avoids the artificial condition where perfect weather forecasts eliminate hedgeable risk. Second, the shortened five-year training period (2018-2022) prevents overfitting to historical patterns while maintaining sufficient data for robust parameter estimation, representing a practical compromise between data availability and model generalization. Third, the systematic calculation of hedge multipliers provides actionable guidance that bridges the gap between statistical coefficients and practical contract implementation.

LIMITATIONS AND CAVEATS

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting these results. The analysis uses single-station temperature measurements which create basis risk relative to demand-weighted regional temperatures. Only two years of test data (2023-2024) may not capture the full range of weather extremes and market conditions. The simplified weather forecast error model applies uniform amplification factors rather than conditional errors that depend on forecast confidence and weather regimes. Hedge effectiveness is evaluated using in-sample regression coefficients rather than true out-of-sample rolling-window calibration. Transaction costs, bid-ask spreads, and counterparty credit considerations are excluded from the economic analysis. The results are specific to Northeast United States markets with heating-dominated load profiles and may not generalize to cooling-dominated southern markets or regions with different weather-load relationships.

STRATEGIC RECOMMENDATIONS

Organizations managing electricity load forecast risk should adopt a multi-layered approach that prioritizes forecast accuracy improvement as the primary risk mitigation strategy. Investments in enhanced weather data services, incorporation of ensemble forecasts, machine learning model refinement, and integration of real-time observational data typically provide higher returns than hedging strategies alone. For residual forecast risks after optimization, weather derivative hedging offers a statistically sound approach to managing systematic weather-related forecast errors, with stronger evidence of effectiveness for HDD contracts than CDD contracts across both markets studied.

Hedge programs should be periodically evaluated for continued effectiveness as weather patterns shift with climate change and as load composition evolves with electrification trends. The cost-benefit analysis should account for both forecast error reduction potential and transaction costs, recognizing that basis risk from imperfect weather-load correlations limits achievable hedge effectiveness. Rolling-window calibration of hedge coefficients ensures that hedging strategies adapt to changing market conditions and weather-load relationships.

FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

This analysis establishes a rigorous framework for evaluating forecast error hedging under realistic conditions, opening several avenues for future research. Extensions could test the methodology across additional geographic markets including southern cooling-dominated regions, western states with different weather patterns, and Texas with its independent grid structure. Alternative hedge instruments including temperature options, load-following contracts, and renewable generation hedges could be evaluated. Dynamic hedge ratio adjustment based on forecast confidence intervals and weather regime classification could improve effectiveness. Joint optimization of forecasting and hedging decisions rather than sequential two-stage approaches could find superior strategies. Field validation with actual derivative contract execution would test whether theoretical hedging benefits translate to realized performance.

CONCLUDING REMARKS

This comprehensive analysis demonstrates that weather derivative hedging using HDD and CDD contracts can provide statistically significant relationships with electricity load forecast errors in Northeast markets. The research confirms that forecast improvement remains the primary strategy for managing load uncertainty, with weather derivative hedging serving a supplementary role for systematic weather-related risks in markets where strong statistical relationships between forecast errors and weather outcomes can be established. The framework developed here provides a foundation for rigorous evaluation of hedge strategies under realistic forecasting conditions and demonstrates the importance of region-specific validation before implementing derivative programs. The hedge multipliers and statistical evidence provide actionable guidance for utilities considering weather derivative programs for forecast risk management.
